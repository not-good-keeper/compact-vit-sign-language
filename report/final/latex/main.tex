\title[Compact factorised ViT for isolated ISL words]{ISL-ViT-Tiny: A Compact Factorised Vision Transformer for Isolated-Word Indian Sign Language Recognition, Evaluated on Held-Out Recording Sessions}

\author[1]{\fnm{Yalamanchi} \sur{Kushal}}
\author[1]{\fnm{Nethi} \sur{Kushala Kumar}}
\author[1]{\fnm{Dhruseek} \sur{Rishi Menon}}

\affil[1]{\orgname{Amrita School of Computing, Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham}, \orgaddress{\city{Coimbatore}, \country{India}}}

\abstract{Isolated-word recognition of Indian Sign Language (ISL) on a wearable device needs a small model and an accuracy figure measured on recording conditions the model has not seen. We present ISL-ViT-Tiny, a factorised spatio-temporal Vision Transformer that reads three detected crops per frame (left hand, right hand, face) over 16 frames, adds 21 hand-joint landmarks per hand to the hand tokens, and is trained on INCLUDE (262 words) after masked-image pretraining on iSign. Evaluation holds out whole INCLUDE recording sessions, identified from camera capture counters. On the 472 held-out clips of the 50 deployed words, with predictions restricted to those words and six-view test-time augmentation, the released 4-bit model (2,069,700 bytes; 3,894,214 parameters) reaches 85.9\% mean top-1 accuracy over three seeds and 96.4\% top-5. Hand landmarks add 12.8 points over a pixel-only control trained with the same recipe, and quantisation-aware training keeps the full-precision accuracy within seed noise. The same recipe scores 98.5\% on a random-video split and 70.1\% on held-out sessions, which quantifies how far conventional splits overstate accuracy on this corpus. A learned super-resolution front end raises 180p accuracy from 61.0\% to 76.0\%, and the integrated application reproduces the offline evaluation clip for clip.}

\keywords{Indian Sign Language recognition, Vision Transformer, spatio-temporal modelling, hand landmarks, 4-bit quantisation, evaluation protocol, super-resolution GAN}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Indian Sign Language is the primary language of a large Deaf community in India; the INCLUDE authors cite more than five million users~\cite{sridhar2020include}. An automatic recogniser for isolated ISL words could support communication with non-signers, and a camera on a pair of smart glasses is one place such a recogniser could run. That target sets two requirements that pull against each other. The network must fit a memory and compute budget far below that of standard video models, and its reported accuracy must describe what happens when the signer, room and lighting differ from the training recordings.

The second requirement is the harder one to meet with public ISL data. INCLUDE, the main labelled word-level corpus, contains 4,287 videos of 263 words recorded by experienced signers~\cite{sridhar2020include}; the copy used here has 4,257 usable clips of 262 words. A word has a median of 15 clips, many of them back-to-back takes of the same signer in the same session. A random split of such data puts near-identical takes on both sides of the train/test boundary, so a high score can reflect recognition of the recording rather than of the sign. The problem is not specific to sign language: Kapoor and Narayanan document train--test leakage across machine-learning-based science~\cite{kapoor2023leakage}. Within sign language, AUTSL reports 95.95\% for its baselines on random splits and 62.02\% on its user-independent split~\cite{sincan2020autsl}. Published INCLUDE results of 85.6\% (263 words) and 94.5\% (INCLUDE-50)~\cite{sridhar2020include}, and 93.5 to 94.7\% for pose-based models~\cite{selvaraj2022openhands}, were obtained on the dataset's own split; Section~\ref{sec:res:protocol} measures how much such splits inflate accuracy on this corpus.

Transformer video models~\cite{arnab2021vivit,bertasius2021timesformer} and pose-based sign recognisers~\cite{bohacek2022spoter,jiang2021samslr,selvaraj2022openhands} give strong results on sign data, and recent work targets edge hardware~\cite{lamaakal2026tinymslr,venkatappa2026kannada}. In the sources we reviewed (Section~\ref{sec:related}) we did not find an ISL word recogniser that reports, in one place, a hard size budget, a measured quantisation cost, a split that holds out whole recording sessions, and an application tested against the offline evaluation.

The project's objectives were to (i) reach at least 75\% top-1 accuracy on 50 deployable words measured on held-out recording sessions; (ii) keep the model near 2\,MB; (iii) use an evaluation protocol that does not reward memorising a session; (iv) integrate the model into a working interface; and (v) test a generative adversarial network (GAN) in a position where its contribution can be isolated. This report describes the implementation at commit \texttt{3330b87} of the public repository and the measurements made on it.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A task-specific compact recogniser: a factorised spatial-then-temporal Transformer over hand and face crops, with crop position, detection status and normalised hand-joint features added to the crop tokens (Section~\ref{sec:method:model}), sized and quantised to a 2\,MB budget.
\item Controlled measurements on one fixed test set: the landmark stream (+12.8 points, paired $p\le 5\times10^{-9}$ in each seed), post-training versus quantisation-aware 4-bit compression, temporal length, crop resolution and training duration (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item A recording-session-aware evaluation built from capture identifiers, with a matched measurement of how much a random split inflates accuracy (about 28 points for one recipe) (Section~\ref{sec:method:eval}).
\item An integrated application whose prediction route reproduces the offline per-clip outcome for three classifier variants and three low-resolution front ends, on 50 held-out clips each (Section~\ref{sec:res:app}).
\item A controlled super-resolution study that separates the benefit of a learned upscaler from the benefit of the adversarial loss (Section~\ref{sec:res:gan}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{ISL datasets and recognisers.} INCLUDE~\cite{sridhar2020include} is the reference labelled corpus for isolated ISL words; its best model, a pretrained feature extractor and encoder with a trained decoder, reached 85.6\% on 263 words and 94.5\% on the 50-word subset. CISLR~\cite{joshi2022cislr} has a vocabulary of about 4,700 words with roughly 1.5 videos per word, so its authors frame recognition as a one-shot task; their prototype baseline reached 16.81\% top-1 on CISLR v1.0-a. iSign~\cite{joshi2024isign} provides a large collection of ISL video--sentence pairs and benchmark tasks. Recent ISL systems include a CNN--ViT conversational agent reporting 97.5\% accuracy under optimised conditions on a domain-specific dataset~\cite{nedungadi2025vit} and a two-handed dynamic ISL recogniser~\cite{govindharajalu2025isl}; Damdoo and Kumar survey the field~\cite{damdoo2025survey}.

\paragraph{Video and pose models for sign recognition.} The Vision Transformer~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} and the data-efficient DeiT training recipe~\cite{touvron2021deit} made patch-token models practical on modest datasets. For video, ViViT~\cite{arnab2021vivit} studies variants that factorise spatial and temporal attention, and TimeSformer~\cite{bertasius2021timesformer} compares divided space--time attention with joint attention. Sign Language Transformers~\cite{camgoz2020slt} applied Transformers to continuous recognition and translation. Pose-based recognisers operate on body and hand keypoints: SPOTER~\cite{bohacek2022spoter} normalises hand poses in a local coordinate frame and reports 63.18\% on WLASL100; SAM-SLR~\cite{jiang2021samslr} fuses skeleton, RGB and depth streams; SignBERT~\cite{hu2021signbert} pretrains a hand-model-aware representation; OpenHands~\cite{selvaraj2022openhands} releases pose-based models for six sign languages and reports 93.5\% (SL-GCN) on INCLUDE, rising to 94.7\% with self-supervised pretraining. Our model combines the two families: pixels of detected regions, plus hand landmarks from MediaPipe~\cite{lugaresi2019mediapipe,zhang2020mphands} added to the visual tokens rather than processed by a separate graph network.

\paragraph{Compact models and quantisation.} Integer quantisation with simulated rounding during training~\cite{jacob2018quant} and the straight-through gradient estimator~\cite{bengio2013ste} are the standard basis of quantisation-aware training; Nagel et al. review post-training and quantisation-aware methods~\cite{nagel2021whitepaper}. Q-BERT~\cite{shen2020qbert} and GPTQ~\cite{frantar2023gptq} give each block of weights its own scale (group-wise scaling). For sign recognition at the edge, TinyMSLR~\cite{lamaakal2026tinymslr} reports fewer than 2.7\,M parameters and 7.2\,MB on a 20-class isolated-sign benchmark built from two corpora with a signer-independent protocol, and a Kannada Sign Language study~\cite{venkatappa2026kannada} deploys quantised LSTM and Transformer models on a smartphone.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocols.} WLASL~\cite{li2020wlasl} collects more than 2,000 words from over 100 signers, and AUTSL~\cite{sincan2020autsl} publishes a user-independent benchmark split and reports the random-versus-user-independent gap quoted in Section~\ref{sec:intro}. INCLUDE ships without signer identifiers; the protocol in Section~\ref{sec:method:eval} reconstructs recording sessions from the camera's running clip counter.

\paragraph{Super-resolution for recognition.} SRGAN~\cite{ledig2017srgan}, ESRGAN~\cite{wang2018esrgan} and Real-ESRGAN~\cite{wang2021realesrgan} sharpen upscaled images by training the generator against a discriminator~\cite{goodfellow2014gan}. Blau and Michaeli show that perceptual quality and distortion measures such as PSNR trade off against each other~\cite{blau2018pd}. Whether super-resolution helps a downstream task is an empirical question: Dai et al. examined it for several vision tasks~\cite{dai2016srhelpful}, and Haris et al. trained super-resolution with a detection loss~\cite{haris2018taskdriven}. Our study asks the narrower question of what the adversarial term adds to learned upscaling for a frozen sign recogniser.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Task definition}\label{sec:method:task}
Each input is a short video containing one isolated sign. The model $f_\theta$ outputs logits over the $K=262$ INCLUDE words it was trained on. The deployed product answers from a fixed subset $\mathcal{D}$ of $|\mathcal{D}|=50$ words, declared before any run from the word list of the deployed split file (\ev{islvit/mask50.py\#L45-L53}{mask50.py:45}). The deployed prediction is
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}=\arg\max_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\ \bar{p}_k,\qquad \bar{\mathbf{p}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\operatorname{softmax}\big(f_\theta(x_v)\big),\label{eq:mask}
\end{equation}
where $x_v$ is view $v$ of the clip and $\mathcal{V}$ is the set of test-time views (Section~\ref{sec:method:metrics}). Restricting the argmax to $\mathcal{D}$ is what a 50-word product does with this network; Section~\ref{sec:res:main} also reports the unrestricted 262-word accuracy.

\subsection{Data and vocabulary}\label{sec:method:data}
The classifier is trained only on INCLUDE~\cite{sridhar2020include} (CC-BY-4.0): 4,257 clips of 262 words at 1920$\times$1080 pixels and 25 frames per second, all from the original 1080p sources. Two other corpora enter as unlabelled or test data. iSign~\cite{joshi2024isign} supplied 17,896 clips (one per source video, after a hand-detection filter) for self-supervised pretraining, and CISLR v1.5-a~\cite{joshi2022cislr} supplied 609 clips of 218 shared words as a cross-corpus test.

\subsection{Region extraction and missing detections}\label{sec:method:pre}
One code path builds the training cache, the test cache and the application input (\ev{islvit/data/crops.py\#L490-L564}{crops.py:490}, reused by \ev{islvit/predict.py\#L42-L57}{predict.py:42}). Thirty-two frames are sampled uniformly from the clip, resized to a height of 720 pixels and letterboxed to 1280$\times$720 (\ev{islvit/data/crops.py\#L163-L204}{crops.py:163}). This resize is also where the super-resolution front end is inserted (Section~\ref{sec:method:sr}). MediaPipe Holistic~\cite{lugaresi2019mediapipe} locates the hands and face; a hand box is a square around the 21 hand landmarks enlarged 1.5 times. When Holistic misses a hand, a region around the forearm predicted by the body pose is upscaled to 256 pixels and passed to a dedicated hand landmarker~\cite{zhang2020mphands} (\ev{islvit/data/crops.py\#L305-L351}{crops.py:305}). If both fail, the nearest real box is carried into the gap and the frame is flagged as interpolated (\ev{islvit/data/crops.py\#L362-L374}{crops.py:362}). Crops are stored at 128 pixels and resized to 64 pixels at model input.

Two observations during development shaped this design. A fallback box synthesised from pose geometry alone can land on clothing or background, so every crop carries a provenance code (direct, region rescue, interpolated, missing) that the model receives as input. MediaPipe's detectors also keep state between videos even in image mode, so the detectors are rebuilt at the start of every video (\ev{islvit/data/crops.py\#L413-L428}{crops.py:413}); the 472 test clips were extracted this way into a separate cache, and every headline number uses that cache.

\subsection{Landmark features}\label{sec:method:lm}
For each hand $h$ and frame $t$, MediaPipe returns joints $\mathbf{J}_{t,h}\in\mathbb{R}^{21\times3}$ in frame-normalised coordinates. The model computes its own invariant features (\ev{islvit/models/isl_vit.py\#L312-L344}{isl_vit.py:312}), so every inference path computes them identically. Let $\tilde{\mathbf{J}}=\mathbf{J}\operatorname{diag}(a,1,1)$ with $a=16/9$, which undoes the letterbox aspect ratio, and let $\tilde{\mathbf{j}}_{0}$ be the wrist row. The handshape descriptor is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_{t,h}=\frac{\operatorname{vec}\big(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{t,h}-\mathbf{1}\,\tilde{\mathbf{j}}^{\top}_{t,h,0}\big)}{\max\!\big(\max_i\lVert(\tilde{\mathbf{j}}_{t,h,i}-\tilde{\mathbf{j}}_{t,h,0})_{xy}\rVert_2,\ 10^{-3}\big)}\in\mathbb{R}^{63},\label{eq:shape}
\end{equation}
which removes hand position and distance from the camera. Sign location is the wrist relative to the shoulder midpoint $\mathbf{c}_t$, divided by the shoulder width $w_t$: $\mathbf{u}_{t,h}=(\tilde{\mathbf{j}}_{t,h,0}-\mathbf{c}_t)_{xy}/w_t\in\mathbb{R}^2$. Seven upper-body pose points expressed in the same body frame, with their visibilities, form a 21-dimensional pose vector $\mathbf{r}_t$. If either shoulder has visibility of 0.3 or less, every body-relative quantity is set to zero. The 65-dimensional hand vector $[\mathbf{s};\mathbf{u}]$ and the pose vector each pass through a two-layer perceptron (Linear--GELU--Linear, 192 units) whose last layer starts at zero, so at the first training step the landmark model computes exactly what the pixel-only model computes.

\subsection{Recognition model}\label{sec:method:model}
Figure~\ref{fig:model} shows the network (\ev{islvit/models/isl_vit.py\#L136-L299}{isl_vit.py:136}). The input is $T=16$ frames $\times$ $S=3$ streams of $64\times64$ RGB crops.

\textit{Spatial encoder.} One encoder with shared weights processes all $T\cdot S=48$ crops (\ev{islvit/models/isl_vit.py\#L87-L133}{isl_vit.py:87}). A $16\times16$ patch embedding gives 16 patch tokens plus a class token, which pass through four pre-norm Transformer blocks of width 192 with three heads; the output class token is the crop embedding $\mathbf{e}_{t,s}\in\mathbb{R}^{192}$. Width and head count match DeiT-Tiny~\cite{touvron2021deit}, so ImageNet and self-supervised weights load without projection.

\textit{Token conditioning.} Cropping discards where a hand is, yet location distinguishes many signs. Each crop token therefore receives additive terms:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{z}_{t,s}={}&\mathbf{e}_{t,s}+\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{str}}_{s}+\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{time}}_{t}+g(\mathbf{b}_{t,s})+(1-d_{t,s})\,\mathbf{m}\\
&+\mathbb{I}[s\in\{\mathrm{L},\mathrm{R}\}]\,q_{t,s}\,\phi_{\mathrm{hand}}([\mathbf{s};\mathbf{u}]_{t,s})\\
&+\mathbb{I}[s=\mathrm{F}]\,o_t\,\phi_{\mathrm{pose}}(\mathbf{r}_t),
\end{aligned}\label{eq:token}
\end{equation}
with learned stream and time embeddings $\mathbf{E}$; box geometry $\mathbf{b}=(x_c,y_c,\text{size})$ mapped by a zero-initialised perceptron $g$; detection indicator $d$ and a learned missing-detection embedding $\mathbf{m}$; hand-present indicator $q$; pose-valid indicator $o$; and landmark projections $\phi$ (Section~\ref{sec:method:lm}). L, R and F denote the left-hand, right-hand and face streams. The landmark terms add 91,008 parameters and no tokens.

\textit{Temporal encoder.} The 48 conditioned tokens and a class token pass through four more Transformer blocks, and a linear layer maps the final class token to 262 logits. Attention therefore runs over 17 tokens inside each crop and 49 tokens across the clip, instead of the $48\times17=816$ tokens that joint space--time attention would process. This is the factorised-encoder arrangement studied in ViViT~\cite{arnab2021vivit}.

\subsection{Training procedure}\label{sec:method:train}
The released model was trained on the 2,845 training clips of the session-disjoint split with validation folded in (Section~\ref{sec:method:eval}), for a fixed 1,000 epochs, keeping the final weights (\ev{islvit/train.py\#L244-L730}{train.py:244}; launch scripts \ev{run_landmarks.sh}{run_landmarks.sh} and \ev{run_lm_s2_resume.sh}{run_lm_s2_resume.sh}). The optimiser is AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} with a peak learning rate of $5\times10^{-4}$, 10 warm-up epochs, cosine decay to 1\%, batch 64 and weight decay 0.05 on weight matrices only; the spatial encoder learns at 0.1 times the base rate. Regularisation uses label smoothing of 0.1~\cite{szegedy2016inception}, mixup with $\alpha=0.2$ on half the batches~\cite{zhang2018mixup}, stochastic depth up to 0.1~\cite{huang2016stochdepth}, gradient clipping at 1.0 and an exponential moving average of the weights. Augmentation applies temporal jitter within uniform segments, scale-jittered crop windows, horizontal flips that swap the two hand streams and their joints, brightness, contrast and per-channel gain changes, greyscale, stream dropout, and a per-clip resolution jitter. Seeds 0, 1 and 2 give three independent models. The effective recipe of each run is stored in its \texttt{summary.json}.

Final weights are kept rather than a best-validation checkpoint, because validation clips come from the training sessions; on the 262-word split this choice raised test top-1 from 22.1\% to 25.3\% (Table~\ref{tab:trace}, row E6).

\subsection{Self-supervised initialisation}\label{sec:method:ssl}
Both encoders are initialised from masked-image pretraining on iSign in the style of SimMIM~\cite{xie2022simmim} (\ev{islvit/pretrain.py\#L182-L258}{pretrain.py:182}; launch \ev{run_isign_pipeline.sh}{run_isign_pipeline.sh}). A learned mask token replaces 75\% of the patch tokens, sampled jointly over time, stream and space; a two-block decoder predicts per-patch-normalised pixels, and the loss is the mean squared error on masked patches. Interpolated crops are excluded from the loss so that the model does not learn to reconstruct stale background. Position and time embeddings are resized when the pretrained geometry differs from the fine-tuning geometry (\ev{islvit/train.py\#L75-L116}{train.py:75}).

\subsection{Four-bit quantisation}\label{sec:method:quant}
Every weight matrix (tensors with at least two dimensions) is split into groups $G$ of 128 consecutive weights and quantised symmetrically (\ev{islvit/export.py\#L223-L238}{export.py:223}):
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}\sigma_G=\operatorname{fp16}\!\Big(\frac{\max_{w\in G}|w|}{7}\Big),\\ c_w=\operatorname{clamp}\big(\operatorname{round}(w/\sigma_G),-8,7\big),\qquad \hat{w}=\sigma_G\,c_w.\end{gathered}\label{eq:int4}
\end{equation}
Two 4-bit codes are packed per byte with one FP16 scale per group; embeddings, normalisation parameters and biases stay in FP16 (\ev{islvit/export.py\#L202-L220}{export.py:202}, \ev{islvit/export.py\#L246-L294}{export.py:246}). For post-training quantisation (PTQ), Eq.~\eqref{eq:int4} is applied once to the trained FP32 weights (\ev{islvit/int4_eval.py}{int4_eval.py}). Quantisation-aware training (QAT) continues training for a fixed 300 epochs at a learning rate of $5\times10^{-5}$, computing the forward pass with $\hat{w}$ and applying the gradients to the full-precision weights, a straight-through estimator~\cite{bengio2013ste,jacob2018quant} implemented by swapping tensors around each step (\ev{islvit/qat.py\#L50-L84}{qat.py:50}; launch \ev{run_lm_qat.sh}{run_lm_qat.sh}). QAT and the exporter call the same target-selection and rounding functions, so the model trained is the model saved. The release files are written by \ev{islvit/release.py}{release.py} and read by \ev{islvit/export.py\#L181-L200}{export.py:181}, which rebuilds FP32 weights from the stored codes at load time.

\subsection{Super-resolution front end and GAN study}\label{sec:method:sr}
For video below 720 pixels in height, the frame reader enlarges frames before detection. The default is bicubic interpolation; the alternative is a $\times4$ generator applied at the same point (\ev{islvit/data/crops.py\#L163-L204}{crops.py:163}). The generator is a compact VGG-style network of the kind distributed with the Real-ESRGAN code base, trained on Real-ESRGAN-style synthetic degradations~\cite{wang2021realesrgan}: 3$\times$3 convolutions at low resolution, a final pixel shuffle, and a residual over nearest-neighbour upsampling (\ev{islvit/sr/models.py\#L32-L48}{sr/models.py:32}). Training pairs come from high-resolution hand, face and frame patches of training sessions only, degraded by random blur, resizing, noise and JPEG compression (\ev{islvit/sr/degrade.py\#L122-L149}{sr/degrade.py:122}). Figure~\ref{fig:gan} shows the comparison design. The arms are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SR-L1 (no GAN)}: a 621,424-parameter generator trained for 100,000 iterations with pixel L1 loss only.
\item \textbf{SR-GAN v1}: SR-L1 fine-tuned for 60,000 iterations with L1, VGG19 perceptual~\cite{johnson2016perceptual,simonyan2015vgg} and adversarial~\cite{goodfellow2014gan} losses against a spectrally normalised U-Net discriminator~\cite{schonfeld2020unetd,miyato2018sn} (\ev{islvit/sr/models.py\#L51-L81}{sr/models.py:51}).
\item \textbf{SR-GAN v2}: v1 trained for 80,000 further iterations with a fresh discriminator.
\item \textbf{SR-GAN v3 and its control}: a 2,039,568-parameter generator with a relativistic average adversarial loss~\cite{jolicoeur2019ragan}, H.264 degradation, and a loss on hand landmarks predicted by a differentiable ResNet-18~\cite{he2016resnet} heatmap network with soft-argmax readout~\cite{sun2018integral} (\ev{islvit/sr/train.py\#L100-L109}{sr/train.py:100}). The control is the same generator and hand loss without the discriminator.
\end{itemize}
Test inputs are the 472 test clips degraded deterministically inside the frame reader by area downscaling and one JPEG encode: 360p (quality 60), 240p (50) and 180p (40) (\ev{islvit/sr/degrade.py\#L35-L43}{sr/degrade.py:35}; launch \ev{run_gan_study.sh}{run_gan_study.sh}, \ev{run_gan_study_v2.sh}{run_gan_study_v2.sh}, \ev{run_gan_v3_test.sh}{run_gan_v3_test.sh}). The three released classifiers are scored unchanged on each front end's output. Before the first test run, the rule for a verdict was fixed: ``helps'' requires a higher mean, all three seeds in the same direction, and pooled exact McNemar $p<0.05$ (\ev{islvit/gan_report.py\#L98-L117}{gan_report.py:98}).

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:method:eval}
INCLUDE file names carry the camera's running clip counter (\texttt{MVI\_xxxx}). Clips of one word whose counters differ by at most 5 form a \emph{take-group} of back-to-back recordings (\ev{islvit/data/splits.py\#L118-L136}{splits.py:118}); the gap histogram is bimodal, and thresholds from 5 to 20 give the same partition. Clustering the counters of the whole corpus with the same gap gives 13 contiguous \emph{session blocks} (\ev{islvit/data/splits.py\#L170-L206}{splits.py:170}), the recording-session structure that INCLUDE's metadata makes available. The session-disjoint split assigns whole blocks to test by random search over 20,000 candidate assignments, scored mainly on how many test words keep at least six training clips (\ev{islvit/data/splits.py\#L209-L299}{splits.py:209}). Blocks 0, 5 and 6 form the test side. Figure~\ref{fig:protocols} contrasts the three protocols.

\subsection{Metrics, test-time views and statistics}\label{sec:method:metrics}
Top-1 and top-5 accuracy are the share of clips whose true word is ranked first or within the first five; balanced accuracy is the mean per-word recall (\ev{islvit/tta.py\#L65-L75}{tta.py:65}). \emph{Masked-50} applies Eq.~\eqref{eq:mask} with $\mathcal{D}$ the deployed words; \emph{wide-262} takes the argmax over all 262 outputs. Test-time augmentation (TTA) averages softmax probabilities over six views: three temporal phases (0.25, 0.5 and 0.75 of each sampling segment), each with and without a horizontal flip that swaps the hand streams (\ev{islvit/tta.py\#L46-L62}{tta.py:46}). Each headline figure is the mean of three seeds. Paired comparisons on identical clips use the exact two-sided McNemar test~\cite{mcnemar1947} on the discordant clips, per seed and pooled over seeds. Five seeds of one earlier configuration gave a top-1 standard deviation of 1.9 points on 1,010 clips (\ev{runs/sd263_ssl_foldval/summary.json}{runs/sd263_ssl_foldval*}); differences below about 5 points between single runs are therefore read as seed noise.

\subsection{Application path}\label{sec:method:app}
The application is a standard-library HTTP server (\ev{islvit/serve.py\#L102-L202}{serve.py:102}) and a seven-screen browser interface built into one page from \evdir{islvit/ui/src}{islvit/ui/src}. \texttt{POST /predict} receives raw video bytes and runs the same extraction, landmark and view functions as the offline evaluation (\ev{islvit/serve.py\#L50-L99}{serve.py:50}). The demo launcher (\ev{islvit/demo.py\#L69-L118}{demo.py:69}) replaces the server's classifier with one that applies the 50-word restriction exactly as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mask}, and can bind a super-resolution generator into the frame reader. The model is chosen with \texttt{--run} at launch. A prediction whose confidence is below a threshold $\tau$ (default 0.40) goes to a screen listing five candidates, and a user's correction is appended to a log. Figure~\ref{fig:system} shows the offline and online paths.

\subsection{Main result}\label{sec:res:main}

The released 4-bit landmark model reached 85.9\% top-1 (seeds 84.5, 87.5 and 85.8), 96.4\% top-5 and 85.8\% balanced accuracy, measured as masked-50 with six-view TTA on the 472 held-out-session test clips and averaged over three independently trained seeds. During this audit the three files in \evdir{release}{release/} were re-scored on the local test cache, and the per-clip outcomes matched the tracked files exactly for all three seeds (\new{report/final/audit/rescore_release_s*.json}).

\paragraph{Landmark stream.} Adding the landmark stream, with nothing else changed, raised top-1 from 73.7\% to 86.5\%. The gain replicated in every seed: 76, 83 and 88 clips gained against 18, 21 and 26 lost ($p=1.2\times10^{-9}$, $6.7\times10^{-10}$ and $4.6\times10^{-9}$). The pipeline already ran MediaPipe to place crop boxes; the landmark model uses joints that the pixel-only model discarded.

\paragraph{Quantisation.} Rounding the FP32 landmark weights to 4 bits after training gave 83.4\%; quantisation-aware training reached 85.9\%. Pooled over seeds, QAT gained 77 clips and lost 41 relative to PTQ ($p=0.0012$). Against FP32, QAT gained 37 clips and lost 45 (pooled $p=0.44$): the released 4-bit model matches its FP32 parent within seed noise.

\section{Discussion and originality}\label{sec:discussion}
% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{What moved accuracy.} The largest single gain came from an input the pipeline already computed: hand joints added 12.8 points for 91,008 parameters. Doubling the frames from 8 to 16 added about 10 points, and training longer helped until 16 frames, then saturated. With about 11 training clips per deployed word (571 clips for 50 words), explicit handshape measurements probably give the network information that 64-pixel crops carry only implicitly, which is consistent with larger crops adding tokens without adding accuracy.

\paragraph{What compression costs.} Group-wise 4-bit storage reduces the weights from about 15.6\,MB to 2.07\,MB. Rounding after training gave 83.4\%; training with the rounding in the loop gave 85.9\%, level with the FP32 model within seed noise. The release file is a storage format; the server rebuilds FP32 weights from it at load time.

\paragraph{What the protocol changes.} A 28-point gap between random-video and session-disjoint splits for one recipe suggests that INCLUDE accuracies reported on random splits largely measure recognition within recording sessions. Published INCLUDE-50 numbers~\cite{sridhar2020include,selvaraj2022openhands} are measured on the dataset's own split and are therefore a different quantity from the 85.9\% measured here on held-out sessions.

\paragraph{What the GAN study shows.} Learned upscaling recovered most of the accuracy lost at 180p, and the adversarial term added a measurable gain at 240p. The 2$\times$2 decomposition shows where the remaining headroom is: after upscaling, most of the gap to clean video lies in where the detector places hand joints. This motivated the v3 generator, trained against a hand-landmark loss, and it raised the share of detected hands at 180p from 68.2\% to 77.6\%.

\paragraph{Limitations.} All headline results come from one INCLUDE test set of 472 clips over 50 words, with three seeds per variant. Recording sessions were reconstructed from camera capture counters rather than taken from dataset metadata. Latency was measured on a desktop CPU, not the target wearable.

\paragraph{Originality.} The components of the system are established methods: factorised video Transformers, masked-image pretraining, MediaPipe landmarks, straight-through quantisation, group-wise scales and GAN super-resolution. The contribution is system-level and experimental. At the system level it is a region-crop factorised Transformer with additive position, detection-status and landmark conditioning, sized and quantised to a 2\,MB file, and served by an application that runs the evaluated code path. At the experimental level it is a set of seed-replicated, controlled measurements of each design lever on one locked test set, a session-aware split with a measured leakage gap, a cross-corpus measurement on CISLR, and a GAN study with a no-GAN control. Table~\ref{tab:contrib} ties each claim to its evidence. Among the works reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:related}, no ISL word recogniser reports these properties together.

\section{Conclusion and future work}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

The project met its targets under the conditions it measured: 85.9\% top-1 on 50 deployed words from held-out INCLUDE sessions, in a 2,069,700-byte 4-bit file, served by an application that reproduces the offline per-clip outcomes. Each design choice rests on controlled comparisons on one locked test set with three seeds. Three next steps extend the work: record the planned signer-disjoint set (three signers, 50 words, three takes; protocol in \ev{docs/CAPTURE_PROTOCOL.md}{docs/CAPTURE_PROTOCOL.md}) and score the released model on it unchanged; train a pose-only graph network on the same split as a second architecture family; and run the model with integer kernels on a phone-class processor, timing the detector and the classifier separately.

\section{Architecture design and model iterations}\label{app:design}

Every number below comes from the main text or from the run file linked beside it; this appendix adds no new measurement.

\subsection{What the architecture borrows}

ISL-ViT-Tiny is assembled from four published designs; Table~\ref{tab:borrow} lists what each contributes and what was changed. The largest borrowing is the \emph{factorised encoder} of ViViT~\cite{arnab2021vivit}. ViViT describes four ways to apply a Transformer to video. Its Model~1 attends jointly over every space--time token; Model~2, the factorised encoder, runs a spatial Transformer on the tokens of each frame, reads out one [CLS] embedding per frame, and passes the sequence of frame embeddings to a temporal Transformer; Models~3 and~4 factorise attention inside each block instead. TimeSformer's divided space--time attention~\cite{bertasius2021timesformer} works like Model~3, alternating temporal and spatial attention within every block. ISL-ViT-Tiny follows Model~2 because no attention layer in it ever spans all the patches of a clip: the spatial stage attends within one image, and the temporal stage sees one token per image.

The change from ViViT is the unit that the spatial stage encodes. ViViT encodes a whole frame; ISL-ViT-Tiny encodes a detected crop, three per frame, so Stage~B receives $T\cdot S=48$ tokens rather than $T=16$ and needs a stream embedding to tell the left hand, the right hand and the face apart. Stage~B therefore attends across time and across body parts in one sequence, so it can relate the two hands to each other and to the face at any pair of frames. The attention cost of this arrangement can be counted directly. Per head and per layer, Stage~A computes $48\times17^2=13{,}872$ attention scores and Stage~B $49^2=2{,}401$, a total of 16,273. Joint attention over the 816 patch tokens of the clip would compute $816^2=665{,}856$, about 41 times as many.

Inside each stage the building blocks are those of ViT~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}: a strided convolution as patch embedding, a learned [CLS] token, a learned position embedding and pre-norm Transformer blocks. The dimensions are those of DeiT-Tiny~\cite{touvron2021deit} (width 192, three heads, MLP width 768), chosen so that ImageNet weights load into Stage~A without a projection layer. The loader copies the patch embedding, the [CLS] token and the first four of the twelve pretrained blocks, and resizes the $14\times14$ position grid to the $4\times4$ grid of a 64-pixel crop by bicubic interpolation (\ev{islvit/models/isl_vit.py\#L347-L377}{isl_vit.py:347}). The same width is what the iSign self-supervised checkpoint was trained at, which is why the model was never made narrower.

The self-supervised initialisation follows SimMIM~\cite{xie2022simmim} rather than the masked autoencoder (MAE) of He et al.~\cite{he2022mae}. The mask hides 75\% of the patches and is drawn jointly over time, stream and space, because a mask drawn inside each crop would let Stage~A solve the task alone and leave Stage~B untrained. MAE removes the masked tokens and encodes only the visible ones. With 16 patches per crop and a mask drawn over the whole clip, that would leave about four visible patches per crop, a different number in each crop. SimMIM instead replaces each masked patch with a learned mask token inside the encoder, so every crop keeps 17 tokens and the encoder that is pretrained is exactly the encoder that is later fine-tuned.

\subsection{What is added, and why}

Cropping removes two things a sign depends on: where the hand is, and its exact shape, which 64 pixels resolve only coarsely. The additions put both back without adding a single token. The box centre and size, a flag for crops that were interpolated rather than detected, a 65-number handshape and wrist-position vector per hand and a 21-number body-pose vector are each mapped to width 192 and added to the crop token they describe (Eq.~\eqref{eq:token}). The attention pattern, the sequence length and the temporal stage are therefore the same as in the pixel-only model. The last layer of every added MLP starts at zero, so at the first training step the network computes exactly what the pretrained pixel-only network computes. The side inputs then enter gradually, which is intended to keep early gradients from disturbing the transferred weights. The landmark features are computed inside the model from raw joint coordinates, so training, offline evaluation and the application cannot compute them differently.

\subsection{How the model reaches 2\,MB}

The architecture fixes the parameter count at 3,894,214; quantisation sets the bytes. Stored in FP32, the weights take $3{,}894{,}214\times4=15{,}576{,}856$ bytes, about 15.6\,MB. For the 3.76\,M-parameter pixel-only model, 8-bit weights still took 3.70\,MB, nearly twice the budget (\ev{islvit/export.py\#L246-L262}{export.py:246}). Making the network smaller was the other route, and it was costly in a specific way: the width cannot fall below 192 without losing both the ImageNet and the self-supervised initialisations. A distillation student with two blocks per stage at width 192 was built for this purpose and measures 1.95\,MB packed (\ev{islvit/distill.py}{distill.py}). No training run of it is recorded: 4-bit weights met the budget with the full model first.

The 4-bit format stores every weight matrix in groups of 128 consecutive weights (Eq.~\eqref{eq:int4}). Each group keeps one FP16 scale, $\sigma_G=\max_{w\in G}|w|/7$, and 128 signed codes in $[-8,7]$, packed two per byte. One group therefore costs $128\times4+16=528$ bits, or 4.125 bits per weight, and the scales add 3.1\% to the codes. A single scale per matrix would be cheaper still, but the largest weight in a $768\times192$ matrix would then set the step size for all 147,456 weights and leave most of them on a few levels; a group of 128 confines each outlier's effect to its own group. Embeddings, LayerNorm parameters and biases stay in FP16. At 4.125 bits per weight the 3.89\,M parameters would need about 2.0\,MB. The released file, 2,069,700 bytes, also holds the FP16 tensors, the configuration and the word list. Relative to FP32 the file is 7.5 times smaller.

Rounding a trained FP32 model this way lowered masked-50 accuracy from 86.5\% to 83.4\% (\ev{runs/f16_262w_lm_s0/ptq4_clean.json}{ptq4_clean.json}). Quantisation-aware training recovers most of the loss. For 300 further epochs the forward pass uses the rounded weights and the gradient updates full-precision master weights through a straight-through estimator~\cite{bengio2013ste,jacob2018quant}; the released model scores 85.9\%, within seed noise of FP32. The scale is rounded to FP16 before the weights are reconstructed, and training and export call the same rounding functions, so the model that is scored is the file that is shipped (\ev{islvit/export.py\#L202-L238}{export.py:202}).

Apart from the landmark inputs, the steps that mattered most were not architectural. Fixing checkpoint selection and folding validation into training (step 2) and training for longer (step 6) changed the recipe, not the network. Self-supervised initialisation helped once the input blur of the pretraining corpus was matched by resolution jitter (step 3). Doubling the frames from 8 to 16 (step 7) was the largest change to the model's input until landmarks. Step 8 changed two things at once, the 32-frame cache and the removal of CISLR clips from training, so its 4-point gain cannot be split between them; a cache with spare frames probably helps because the sampler can then jitter within each segment. Training on all 262 words and restricting the output to 50 at test time (step 9) scored 73.7\% against 72.7\% for the 50-word models of step 8 under the same six-view protocol, a difference within seed noise, and it keeps the other 212 words available. The hand landmarks (step 10) added 12.8 points for 91,008 parameters, more than every earlier change to resolution, capacity or schedule.

Several candidates were measured and dropped. A 96-pixel input lowered 16-frame accuracy from 66.1\% to 61.0\% (\ev{runs/cap_16f96/summary.json}{cap_16f96}, one seed). Pretraining natively on 16-frame clips reached a lower reconstruction loss but transferred worse, 60.0\% against 70.1\% (\ev{runs/f16_mim16_s0/summary.json}{f16_mim16_s0}). Fine-tuning the 262-word model on the 50-word softmax moved two seeds significantly in opposite directions (\ev{runs/f16_262w_s0_narrow/summary.json}{f16_262w_s*_narrow}). Five further landmark variants, screened on validation clips with the test set untouched, gave no gain that held across seeds. The first two of these decisions rest on one seed each.
